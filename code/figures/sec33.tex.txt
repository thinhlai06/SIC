\section{Modeling}
\label{sec:modeling}

This section reports what the four models learned and how they behave on the two test sets. All numbers come from the saved predictions of the runs in Table~\ref{tab:experiments}; the tables in this section are generated by script from these predictions, not typed by hand. Unless stated otherwise, the models are the 5-epoch runs, and the decision rule is the arg-max of the five class scores (the rule declared before training). Confidence intervals are 95\% paired bootstrap intervals over test images (1{,}000 resamples, shared across models) for the seed-0 runs.

\subsection{Training Dynamics}
\label{subsec:training_dynamics}

Figure~\ref{fig:curves} shows the EyePACS-validation QWK, macro-F1 and cross-entropy after each epoch for all six runs. These are the only curves used to choose the checkpoint; the best epoch was epoch 5 for every run.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/t01_curves_all.jpg}
    \caption{EyePACS-validation QWK (left), macro-F1 (middle) and cross-entropy (right) per epoch for B1 (three seeds, grey), B2 (orange), M1 (blue) and M2 (green). Lines are runs; markers are epoch-end evaluations.}
    \label{fig:curves}
\end{figure}

In the left panel all curves rise during the five epochs and none has flattened, so the models are not fully converged. The ResNet-50 runs reach a validation QWK of 0.692--0.700, M1 reaches 0.694, B2 0.668 and M2 0.662. The three ResNet-50 seeds end within 0.008 of each other, which indicates how much seed noise can be expected. M1 starts higher than B2 (QWK 0.587 versus 0.533 after epoch 1) and ends with the highest validation macro-F1 of all runs (0.535, against 0.496 for B2), which is consistent with a better starting point from retinal pre-training; M2 learns more slowly in the last epochs, as expected from its harder, augmented training images. The validation cross-entropy is not monotone for some runs (epochs 2--3) because the learning rate is at its peak after warm-up. Section~\ref{subsec:budget_check} examines how much a longer schedule changes the picture.

\subsection{Why the In-Domain to Out-of-Domain Difference Needs Care}
\label{subsec:matched}

A negative $\Delta$QWK does not mean that the models generalise better to DDR than to EyePACS. QWK depends on the grade mix of the test set: DDR has 50\% of grade~0 and 35.8\% of grade~2, whereas EyePACS has 73.6\% of grade~0 (Section~\ref{subsec:eda_labels}). DDR labels also come from a consensus of several graders and are probably cleaner than the single-grader EyePACS labels. To remove the first effect, the EyePACS-test images were resampled (stratified by grade) to the DDR grade mix before computing the QWK, and the bootstrap was repeated. Table~\ref{tab:matched} gives the result.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Prior-matched comparison: QWK on EyePACS-test resampled to the DDR grade mix, QWK on DDR-test, and the matched $\Delta$QWK with 95\% bootstrap intervals (seed-0 runs; B1 shown for seed~0).}
    \label{tab:matched}
    \small
    \input{tables/table_matched.tex}
\end{table}

After matching the grade mix, M1 and B1 have a $\Delta$QWK close to zero (interval includes 0), whereas B2 ($-0.061$) and M2 ($-0.073$) still score clearly \emph{higher} on DDR than on the grade-matched EyePACS images. Figures~\ref{fig:inout} and~\ref{fig:bootstrap} display the same information.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/e04_inout.jpg}
    \caption{QWK in-domain (solid bars) and out-of-domain (light bars) with 95\% bootstrap intervals for the four models (5-epoch runs).}
    \label{fig:inout}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/e05_bootstrap.jpg}
    \caption{Left: QWK on DDR-test with 95\% bootstrap intervals. Right: paired bootstrap differences of QWK on DDR-test; an interval that does not contain zero (red dashed line) indicates a difference larger than test-set sampling noise.}
    \label{fig:bootstrap}
\end{figure}

The paired differences in the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap} give the following picture for the QWK on DDR. M1 is \emph{lower} than B2 by 0.031 (interval $-0.051$ to $-0.013$), and not distinguishable from B1 ($-0.014$, interval $-0.034$ to $+0.007$). M2 is higher than M1 by 0.043 (interval $+0.028$ to $+0.058$) and not distinguishable from B2 ($+0.012$, interval $-0.005$ to $+0.030$). For the difference in $\Delta$QWK, M1 has a larger (less negative) value than B2 by 0.058 (interval 0.037 to 0.079) and a similar value to B1 (difference 0.006, interval $-0.017$ to $+0.028$); M2 has a smaller value than M1 by 0.066 (interval $-0.082$ to $-0.049$). A smaller $\Delta$QWK for B2 and M2 simply reflects that these two models are weaker in-domain (0.654 and 0.658) while being as good as or better than M1 on DDR; it is not evidence of better robustness. These intervals measure test-sampling noise only, because B2, M1 and M2 have a single training seed.

\subsection{Per-Grade Behaviour}
\label{subsec:per_grade}

    \label{fig:confusion}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Recall per ICDR grade on DDR-test (5-epoch runs).}
    \label{tab:recall}
    \small
    \input{tables/table_recall.tex}
\end{table}

The matrices show the same structure for all models: grades~0 and~4 are recognised best, grade~1 (mild) is almost never recognised, and grade~2 and~3 are mutually confused. The two ViT-L models with ImageNet-style or augmented training, B2 and M2, never predict grade~1 on DDR (recall 0.000) and have a very high grade-0 recall (0.958 and 0.926); B1 and M1 predict grade~1 sometimes (recall 0.152 and 0.122) at the cost of grade-0 recall (0.802 and 0.710) because they send more healthy eyes to grade~1. Since DDR contains many grade-0 images, a model that avoids false positives on grade~0 earns a higher QWK even though it detects fewer mild cases. This is one reason why B2 and M2 have a higher QWK on DDR than M1 despite a lower macro-F1 on EyePACS. M1 is best on severe disease (grade~4 recall 0.807, compared with 0.679--0.789 for the others) and M2 on grade~3 (0.620).

\subsection{Referable Diabetic Retinopathy}
\label{subsec:referable}

For screening, the practically important question is whether an eye needs referral (grade $\geq 2$). Figure~\ref{fig:roc} shows the ROC curves of the four models on both test sets, computed from the total probability of grades 2--4.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/e02_roc.jpg}
    \caption{ROC curves for referable DR (grade $\geq 2$) on EyePACS-test (left) and DDR-test (right).}
    \label{fig:roc}
\end{figure}

The curves are close to each other, and the AUC values in Table~\ref{tab:main_secondary} (0.872--0.899 on EyePACS, 0.878--0.908 on DDR) confirm that the choice of model matters less for the binary referral decision than for the fine-grained five-grade score. M2 has the highest AUC on DDR (0.908) and B1 on EyePACS (0.899); M1 is the lowest on DDR (0.878).

\subsection{Effect of the Decision Rule}
\label{subsec:decision_rule}

The arg-max rule ignores the ordering of the grades. As a secondary analysis, the five probabilities were converted into an expected grade, and four thresholds on it were fitted on \emph{EyePACS-validation only} to maximise QWK; no DDR image was used. Table~\ref{tab:ordinal} shows the result.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{QWK with ordinal decoding (expected grade and thresholds fitted on EyePACS-validation). B1 is the mean over three seeds.}
    \label{tab:ordinal}
    \small
    \input{tables/table_ordinal.tex}
\end{table}

Every model gains between 0.02 and 0.05 QWK (for example M1 on DDR: 0.691 $\rightarrow$ 0.727, B2: 0.722 $\rightarrow$ 0.744, M2: 0.734 $\rightarrow$ 0.753). The ordering on DDR is unchanged (M2 $>$ B2 $>$ B1 $>$ M1), so the conclusions of this report do not depend on the decision rule; in-domain, B1 is the best model with the ordinal rule (0.731 versus 0.717 for M1). The absolute level of 0.70--0.75 remains below the 0.80 and higher that high-resolution systems report; the likely causes are the low input resolution (224 pixels, in which small microaneurysms are only a few pixels wide), the short training schedule, and the noise in the EyePACS labels.

\subsection{Interpretability}
\label{subsec:interpretability}

Grad-CAM was computed for the last transformer block of M1 and B2 on DDR images: three images that the model classified correctly and three that it classified wrongly, with the same images shown for both models (Figure~\ref{fig:gradcam}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/a01_gradcam.jpg}
    \caption{Grad-CAM on DDR-test images. Top: image with reference grade; middle: M1 heat map and prediction; bottom: B2 heat map and prediction. The first three columns are correct cases of M1, the last three are wrong cases.}
    \label{fig:gradcam}
\end{figure}

For M1 the heat is concentrated on a few small, isolated spots, several of them with a strong peak, while the heat maps of B2 are much broader and cover large parts of the retina, including the periphery and the border of the field of view, so they are less specific. For the correct M1 cases the peaks lie inside the retina rather than on the black border, which is the minimum expected; but they do not always coincide with the visible lesions (for example the bright exudate in the third column is not the strongest region), so the maps should not be read as lesion detections. Among the wrong cases, the second one is a very bright, washed-out image (reference grade~2, predicted grade~0 by both models) and the third a dark image with little lesion contrast; both are examples where image quality, not only pathology, drives the prediction. These maps are qualitative; Grad-CAM is known to be coarse for ViT patches of $16\times16$ pixels and has not been validated against lesion annotations here.

Figure~\ref{fig:tsne_domain} shows a t-SNE embedding of the encoder features of 1{,}500 random test images of each dataset, coloured by dataset, and Figure~\ref{fig:tsne_grade} the same embedding coloured by grade.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/a03_tsne_domain.jpg}
    \caption{t-SNE of the encoder features coloured by dataset (blue: EyePACS-test, orange: DDR-test) for B1, B2, M1 and M2.}
    \label{fig:tsne_domain}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/a04_tsne_grade.jpg}
    \caption{The same t-SNE embeddings coloured by DR grade.}
    \label{fig:tsne_grade}
\end{figure}

In the B1 and B2 embeddings the two datasets overlap strongly; in the M1 and M2 embeddings the two datasets form separate clusters. This was quantified as the fraction of the five nearest neighbours of a point (in feature space) that belong to the other dataset: 0.226 (B1), 0.216 (B2), 0.029 (M1) and 0.034 (M2) (chance would be about 0.5). Thus the retina-specific pre-training \emph{does not} make the features domain-invariant; the features of RETFound encode the acquisition domain (camera, colour, field of view) more strongly than those of the ImageNet models. This result runs against the intuitive expectation behind hypothesis H1 and agrees with the lower DDR scores of M1.

\label{subsec:hypotheses_summary}

In short, under the controlled conditions of this study (one source, one target, 224 pixels, five epochs) the retina-specific pre-training did not translate into a better QWK on the unseen DDR dataset. The domain-shift augmentation was the only change that raised the absolute DDR score of a ViT-L model, at the price of a lower in-domain score. Possible explanations and their consequences are discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:projected_impact}.
